This repository contains my work for CS-250 Software Development Lifecycle at Southern New Hampshire University.

Throughout this course, I learned how Agile and Scrum are used to plan, develop, and test software while handling changing requirements. I also learned how much communication and customer feedback affect the development process.

# Portfolio Reflection

# Understanding User Needs

Working through the Product Owner role changed the way I think about developing software. My first instinct used to be thinking about how I would build a feature. I realized that understanding what the customer actually wants should come before thinking about the technical solution. Creating user stories helped me focus on the user's perspective, organize the product backlog, and prioritize the features that would provide the most value. It also made it easier to see why Agile puts so much emphasis on customer feedback, since priorities can change throughout development.

# My Development Process

Before taking this course, I mostly thought of software development as writing code. I can see now that development starts long before any code is written. Planning, gathering requirements, communicating with stakeholders, testing, and reviewing progress all play an important role in building successful software.

The Agile practice I see myself using the most is breaking larger projects into smaller, more manageable pieces. It makes projects feel less overwhelming, gives me a chance to review my progress more often, and makes it easier to adjust when requirements change. Instead of waiting until the end of a project to discover problems, I'd rather catch them early and make small improvements along the way.

# Being a Good Team Member

Working through each Scrum role also showed me that being a good team member isn't just about writing good code. Every role depended on the others, and it became clear that communication is what keeps a project moving forward. Whether someone is gathering requirements, writing code, testing the application, or helping remove roadblocks, everyone contributes to the final product.

I also realized that being a good teammate means being willing to ask questions, share progress, and accept feedback from others. Software projects change constantly, and no one has every answer. Being open to different ideas and communicating early helps prevent small problems from turning into much bigger ones later.

By the end of this course, I had a much better understanding of what software development actually looks like outside of writing code. I'll continue using these ideas throughout the rest of my degree, in personal projects, and as I work toward a career in game development.
